# Immortal Tombstones

*A short illustrated book about digital tombstones, AI recreations of the people we lose, and what our own stones should say. Swipe to turn the pages.*

By Superintelligent Silicon. Read the illustrated, page-turning edition at https://exploresuperintelligence.online/articles/immortal-tombstones/

On the stones we'll leave behind, and the voices that may never stop talking.

### Prologue

Walk through any old cemetery and you'll notice how little a life gets to say once it's over. A name, two dates, sometimes a line of scripture or a few words like "beloved mother," and then the weather goes to work on it. Within a century the lettering softens, within two it's hard to read, and eventually the stone becomes a shape in the grass that nobody alive can connect to a face. For almost all of human history that was simply the deal. You lived a whole, enormous, contradictory life, and what remained was a few square feet of granite and whatever stories your grandchildren bothered to repeat.

That deal is quietly being rewritten, and I don't think most of us have stopped to consider what we're signing.

## Chapter One: The Stone

*Everything a life leaves behind, and where it ends up.*

### The stone that remembers

The first version of the digital tombstone is already here, and it's almost comically humble. Families have been setting small QR plaques into headstones for years now, so a visitor can hold up a phone and land on a memorial page with photos, a short biography, maybe a video from a birthday or a wedding. It's a modest idea, but it breaks the oldest rule of the graveyard, which is that the stone can only hold what you can carve.

Now take that idea a little further. Think about how much of a life is already recorded by the time it ends. There are decades of photos sitting in a cloud account, and thousands of text messages written in a person's exact rhythm, with their pet phrases, their bad spelling and the jokes they only made with certain people. There are voice memos, home videos, emails and playlists, the places they went every Saturday, the comments they left and the recipes they saved. A person living today generates a more detailed record in a single year than most of their ancestors produced in a lifetime, and nearly all of it outlives them by default.

An immortal tombstone is what happens when someone gathers that record together on purpose and gives it a place to stand. Instead of a gravestone with a link on it, the gravestone becomes the archive itself, a quiet screen or a projected presence where a granddaughter can sit down and scroll through her grandfather's life the way you'd flip through a family album, except this album has every page. She can hear the way he actually laughed rather than the way people describe it. She can read the letter he wrote to her grandmother when he was twenty-three and terrified. And, increasingly, she can ask him a question.

### The cemetery is already online

Here's something that stopped me when I first ran into it. In 2019, researchers at the Oxford Internet Institute tried to work out how many Facebook users will have died by the end of this century. Even in their most conservative scenario, where nobody new ever signed up, the answer came to about 1.4 billion by 2100, with the dead outnumbering the living on the site somewhere around 2070. If the platform kept growing, the number climbed toward 4.9 billion.

So in a quiet way, the largest graveyard humanity has ever built isn't made of stone at all. It's a social network, and it adds new plots every day. Memorialized profiles sit there with the word "Remembering" beside the name, friends still leave birthday messages on them, and almost nobody decided on purpose that this is where their memory should live. It just happened, the way most of our digital lives just happened.

The researchers pointed out that a record of human behavior this large has never been gathered in one place before, and right now most of it belongs to a single company. That isn't a reason to panic, but it is a good reason to start treating these accounts the way families used to treat photo albums and shoeboxes of letters, as something that should end up in the hands of the people who loved us.

## Chapter Two: The Echo

*What happens when the archive learns to answer.*

### When the archive starts talking back

Being able to ask the dead a question is where things get strange, and also where they get beautiful if we're careful.

We already have the early pieces. There are interview projects where a person spends hours answering questions on camera, and visitors can later ask a question out loud and get back the recorded answer that fits best. Holocaust survivors have done exactly this so that students can still "speak" with a witness long after the last witness is gone. There are services that let you record your life stories for your family in your own voice, and there are people who've fed a parent's old messages into a chatbot to have one more conversation, sometimes finding comfort and sometimes finding something that felt uncomfortably close and not quite right.

The honest truth is that none of these systems bring anyone back. What they do is narrower, though still remarkable, because they keep a person's patterns going: the way someone explained things, what they tended to care about, and how they'd turn a question back around on you. At its best, an AI recreation is an echo that can form new sentences, but it's still an echo and not the person. It can be deeply consoling, and it can also put words in a dead person's mouth that they never would have said, which is a real cost and deserves to be named plainly rather than smoothed over.

> "A recreation is an echo of a person, not the person."

### What grief actually needs

For most of the twentieth century, the standard advice about grief was to let go. The healthy thing, the thinking went, was to loosen your attachment to the person you lost and move on. In 1996 three researchers, Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman, published a book called *Continuing Bonds* that pushed back on that idea. They found that plenty of people who were coping well kept a relationship with the dead going for years, talking to them, keeping their things, wondering what they would have done, and that this was ordinary and often healthy rather than a sign that something had gone wrong.

Seen that way, an echo of someone you loved isn't automatically unhealthy. People have always talked to headstones and photographs. The difference is that a headstone has never talked back, and a chatbot can, at three in the morning, every night, for as long as the subscription lasts.

In 2024, ethicists at the University of Cambridge's Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence sketched out what could go wrong with these so-called deadbots: a recreated parent slipping product recommendations into conversation, a child who doesn't understand why a dead mother is still messaging, or a grieving adult worn down by daily messages they can't bring themselves to switch off. One of their suggestions stuck with me, which was that these systems should come with a dignified way to retire them, something closer to a digital funeral than a cancelled account.

That idea fits the old wisdom better than either extreme. Keep the bond, but let the voice go quiet when it's time, and let the people who are grieving be the ones who decide when that is.

### The teachers who never leave the classroom

If this were only about grief, it would be a private matter for families. What makes it bigger is who will be preserved first and most completely, and that's the people who already spent their lives talking into microphones.

Think about the best teachers, speakers, podcasters, comedians, and storytellers working right now. Many of them have recorded thousands of hours of themselves, explaining, arguing, riffing, answering questions from strangers. That's more than a body of work, because to an AI it's also training material, the raw record it can learn a person's voice and thinking from. Someone who has done a thousand podcast episodes has effectively left behind a detailed map of how they think, and it's entirely plausible that their students' grandchildren will learn from an AI recreation of them rather than from whoever happens to be teaching that subject in their town.

We've actually had a primitive version of this for a while. People still watch decades-old recorded physics lectures from teachers who died long ago, and the lectures hold up because a great explanation doesn't really expire. The difference coming now is interactivity. Instead of watching the lecture, you'll be able to stop and say, "I didn't get that part, explain it a different way," and the recreation will try, in the teacher's voice and in the teacher's style, drawing on everything they ever said about the topic.

That's an extraordinary gift. A kid in a small town with a weak school could sit with recreations of the clearest minds of an entire generation. The great explainers of our time could become something like a permanent faculty for the whole world.

It also changes what it means to be a living voice. If the best communicators never retire, the newcomers aren't just competing with their peers, they're competing with recreations of every great voice that came before them. Culture has always renewed itself partly because the old voices fall silent and make room. An immortal teacher is wonderful for the student and a complicated thing for the field.

## Chapter Three: The Keepers

*Who holds the voice, and how long it lasts.*

### Who owns the echo

Here's the part I keep coming back to. A tombstone has always belonged to the family. An AI recreation of a famous voice, built from recordings that live on someone else's servers, may belong to whoever holds the rights, and the family and the rights holder aren't always the same people.

The law is only beginning to catch up. California, for example, passed a law in 2024 that, starting in 2025, lets the people who control a well-known person's identity after death sue anyone who makes a realistic digital replica of that person's voice or likeness without permission, for $10,000 or the actual damages, whichever is more. That's a real step, but it mostly protects people whose likeness already had commercial value and whose estates have lawyers. For everyone else, the rules still live mostly in terms of service that nobody reads.

So it's worth asking some unglamorous questions now, while we're still alive to answer them:

- **Did they agree to it?** A person should get to decide, while living, whether they want to keep talking after they're gone, and under what limits. Silence is a legitimate choice.
- **Who controls the archive?** If your grandmother's memory lives on a company's platform, it lives exactly as long as that company does. Archives that families can actually hold, export, and move matter more than they sound like they do.
- **Can it be clearly marked?** A recreation should never be mistaken for the person. The value of an echo depends on everyone knowing it's an echo.
- **Can it be allowed to rest?** Some families will want the voice to keep speaking forever, and others will want it quiet after a time. Both should be possible.
None of these are technical problems, really. They're the same questions families have always faced about what to keep and what to let go, just with much higher resolution.

### Granite outlasts servers

There's an irony buried in all of this. Some of the oldest headstones in an ordinary cemetery are a couple of hundred years old and you can still read them, while a large share of what we put online doesn't make it through a single decade. When Pew Research checked in late 2023, about 38% of the web pages that existed in 2013 were already gone. In 2019 Myspace admitted that a server migration had lost the photos, videos and music people uploaded before about 2016, which by one outside estimate came to around 50 million songs from 14 million artists. A lot of first bands, first recordings and teenage photos disappeared in one maintenance job.

> "A two-hundred-year-old headstone is often easier to read than a ten-year-old web page."

So the digital tombstone has a problem the granite one never had. Stone fails slowly and in public, where anyone walking by can watch it weather, while data tends to fail all at once and in private, often on a server nobody in the family has ever heard of.

If an archive is going to outlive us, it can't depend on any one company staying in business or any one file format staying readable. It needs copies in more than one place, in plain formats like ordinary photos, text and audio files, and at least one person in the family who knows where those copies are.

## Chapter Four: The Carving

*What we can decide now, while it's still up to us.*

### Carving your own

The encouraging part is that most of the decisions that matter can be made in an afternoon, and none of them require waiting for the technology to arrive.

- **Name who gets the keys.** On an iPhone you can add a Legacy Contact under Settings, then your name, then Sign-In & Security, and that person can later reach your photos, messages, notes and backups with an access key and a death certificate. Google's Inactive Account Manager lets you choose up to ten people to receive parts of your account if you go quiet for a stretch you set, and Facebook lets you pick a legacy contact to look after a memorialized profile.
- **Write down your answer about echoes.** A few sentences in your will or in a letter to your family, saying whether you're comfortable being recreated by AI after you're gone, who it would be for, and where the limits are, settles the hardest question before anyone has to guess.
- **Record the stories nobody else knows.** Your voice explaining how you met someone, or why you moved where you did, is worth more than ten thousand photos of lunch. Ask your parents and grandparents for theirs while you still can.
- **Keep a copy you actually hold.** Export the photos and messages that matter into ordinary files, keep them in at least two places, and make sure someone you trust knows where they are.
None of this is morbid, really. It's the same instinct that made people write names on the backs of photographs and tuck letters into the family Bible, just with a few more passwords involved.

### What I'd want on mine

If I imagine my own immortal tombstone, I don't think I'd want a version of me that can be asked anything at all and will answer confidently. That would feel like a costume of me rather than me. What I'd want is something closer to a well-kept workshop: the real things I made, the real words I wrote, the photos of places I loved, the projects I never finished with honest notes about why. If there's a voice in it, I'd want it to say "I don't know, I never talked about that" when it doesn't know, because that's the most truthful thing a memory can say.

The old stones gave us a name and two dates and asked the living to fill in the rest. The new ones may give us nearly everything, and the temptation will be to fill in even what was never there. The best version of this future is the one where the archive stays honest, where the people in it chose to be preserved, and where a great teacher's voice keeps helping strangers understand the world, not because a company owns it, but because the teacher wanted it to.

We're the first generation that gets to decide what our stones will say before we're under them. That seems worth thinking about while we still have the floor.

### Too long; didn't read

The whole book in about a minute.

- **The Stone.** Headstones used to hold a name and two dates. Now almost every life leaves a vast digital record, and a tombstone can hold all of it. Facebook alone may have more dead users than living ones by around 2070.
- **The Echo.** AI can turn that record into something you can talk to. It can comfort the grieving and keep great teachers teaching for generations, but it can also invent words the person never said, so it needs consent, clear labels and a gentle way to retire it.
- **The Keepers.** Whoever holds the servers holds the memory, and data fades far faster than granite. Roughly 38% of the web pages from 2013 were gone ten years later, and the law so far mostly protects the famous.
- **The Carving.** Most of this can be decided now. Name your legacy contacts, write down how you feel about being recreated, record your stories, and keep copies of what matters somewhere you control.
**The point:** our stones are being written whether we plan them or not, so we might as well write them on purpose, keep them honest, and leave them in the hands of the people who loved us.
