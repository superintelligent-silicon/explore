# We’ll Be Right Back

*Podcasts were going to replace the networks. Somewhere along the way, a lot of them became one.*

By Superintelligent Silicon. Read the illustrated, page-turning edition, with part two (The Ad Ledger) and part three (The Long Pitch), at https://exploresuperintelligence.online/articles/well-be-right-back/

## Prologue: The Best Part

*Right when the story gets good.*

You know the moment. A guest has spent twenty minutes building toward something, the story finally turns, the host leans in, and you can feel the good part coming. Then the voice changes, a little music comes up, and you're hearing about a mattress, a meal kit or a sports betting app. By the time the conversation comes back the spell is broken, and sometimes the host has to remind everyone where they were.

Plenty of longtime listeners remember a different shape. A long-form show would read a few sponsors at the top, maybe one more somewhere in the middle, and then let people talk for hours. Some of the best mentions weren't really ads at all, just a host saying they liked something, whether or not you believed them. It felt a lot closer to sitting in on a conversation than to watching television.

Today it can feel like there's a break every five or ten minutes, and it can feel like the breaks know exactly when you're most hooked. Both of those feelings deserve to be checked against the evidence rather than just repeated, because some of it turns out to be exaggerated and some of it turns out to be more deliberate than most people suspect.

### What this book tries to do

It starts with the TV networks that podcasts were supposed to replace, then counts the actual minutes, opens up the machinery that decides where an ad lands and follows the money behind the biggest deals. After that it gets into the harder ground of mentions that sound organic but may not be. It ends with what listeners can do about all of it, what happens when they do, and whether there's room in this market for something that doesn't interrupt you at all.

**In short**

- The complaint is familiar: ads arrive more often than they used to and seem to land right when a conversation gets good.
- Some of that feeling holds up against the data and some doesn't, and the parts that hold up are often more deliberate than they look.

## Chapter One: The Network They Replaced

*Sixty years of the ad break.*

Commercial breaks have been growing for as long as there's been commercial television. In the 1960s, a typical hour-long American show ran about 51 minutes once you took the ads out. By 2013 a similar hour held about 42 minutes of show, and an ordinary half hour came down to around 22 minutes of programming and 8 of ads. Reruns of older shows sometimes get trimmed by several minutes just to fit the modern breaks.

Viewers noticed every step. When ABC added a fourth minute of commercials to the original Batman series in 1966 to cover its production costs, it started a real fight, and local stations worried out loud that it would become the new normal. It did, and the breaks kept growing from there. By the late 2010s, something like fifteen minutes of commercials in an hour was considered about average on cable.

### The promise of something different

Podcasts grew up as the opposite of all that. Anyone with a microphone could publish, nobody needed a network's permission, and the earliest hit shows were known for long, unhurried conversations that no broadcaster would have allowed. Many hosts built their whole identity around that freedom. They didn't answer to executives, they didn't need anyone's approval, and they'd tell you so, often as a direct contrast to the legacy media their listeners had already tuned out.

Then the money arrived, and fast. US podcast advertising brought in about $106 million in 2015. In 2025 it reached about $2.86 billion, up almost 18 percent in a single year, according to the Interactive Advertising Bureau (IAB), the ad industry's trade group, and the accounting firm PwC. Over the same year, traditional TV advertising fell by more than 13 percent by the IAB's count. That's roughly 27 times as much money in ten years, and money on that scale doesn't just pay for a show. It brings ad sales teams, revenue targets, an inventory of ad slots to fill, and a steady pressure to find one more slot to sell.

None of that is a moral failure on its own. Shows cost money to make, and ads are how most of them pay their editors and producers. But the business that grew up around podcasting started to look like the business it was supposed to replace, and the format followed the money the way formats always have.

> Money on that scale doesn't just pay for a show. It brings ad sales teams.

**In short**

- TV breaks grew from about 9 minutes an hour in the 1960s to around 15 or more, and every new minute was fought over.
- Podcasts sold themselves as the alternative, then US podcast ad revenue grew from about $106 million in 2015 to $2.86 billion in 2025.
- With that money came the same machinery of sales targets and inventory that shaped television.

## Chapter Two: Counting the Minutes

*What the data says, and where it disagrees with the feeling.*

Before getting angry it's worth counting, because the numbers are more mixed than the feeling. An analysis by the measurement site PodRanker found that the median podcast episode spent about 3.7 percent of its runtime on ads, which works out to roughly two and a quarter minutes in a typical hour, and about 13 percent of the episodes it sampled had no ads at all. By that measure the median podcast still carries around an eighth of the commercial load of broadcast TV or radio.

The other end of the curve is where the complaint lives. In the same analysis, nearly one in five episodes went over 10 percent ads, and the heaviest reached 27.6 percent, more than a quarter of its runtime. A separate count from ZeroAds, a company that sells ad removal and says so up front, measured 10,881 recent episodes across 1,112 shows in July and August 2026, drawn from shows its own users subscribe to, which tilts toward popular ones. Its median was 4.2 minutes of ads per episode, about 9.8 percent of runtime, but one episode in ten carried more than ten minutes, and some hour-long shows ran 12 to 13 minutes of ads in every episode.

### So why does it feel worse?

Listeners clearly feel it. In The Podcast Study 2026, a national survey of 1,205 US listeners released in October, 54.8 percent said podcasts already carry too many ads, about one in five said advertising had made them listen to fewer podcasts, and only 8.6 percent said they could tolerate a few more ads to keep shows free. The researchers called it a flashing yellow light.

A few things explain the gap between the median and the mood. PodRanker's analysis found that network ownership tilts shows toward heavier ad loads, and the big shows people actually spend time with tend to sit on networks with sales teams and insertion tools. Many people now watch podcasts on YouTube, where the platform's own mid-roll ads, the ones that cut into the middle of a video, can sit on top of the show's sponsor reads, so one conversation carries two layers of selling. And because ads are now stitched in at download, an old episode can arrive carrying this week's sponsors, so a back catalog that used to be clean keeps getting refilled.

The fair conclusion is that most podcasts are not breaking every five minutes, but the shows with the biggest audiences often break more than they used to, the breaks are less predictable, and the people who listen most are the ones who notice. ZeroAds also argues that in its sample, ad time per episode has stayed fairly flat while industry revenue keeps climbing. Other trackers have measured ad loads rising since 2021, so the growth most likely comes from a mix of more listening, better-targeted and pricier ads, and somewhat more minutes.

**In short**

- The median podcast carries roughly two to four minutes of ads per episode, far less than TV.
- But one episode in ten runs more than ten minutes of ads, and the biggest shows skew heavier.
- More than half of listeners say there are already too many ads, and fewer than one in ten would accept more.

## Chapter Three: Behind the Break

*How an ad decides where to land.*

Most podcast ads today aren't recorded into the episode at all. They're added by a system called dynamic ad insertion, which stitches ads into the audio file at the moment you download or stream it. The episode is published with markers where breaks can go, and when you press play a server picks which ads fill them based on things like where you are, what device you're using and which campaigns still need to be heard a set number of times (each play is called an impression). Two people listening to the same episode can hear different ads in different amounts, and an episode recorded years ago can carry an ad that was sold this morning.

That technology now runs the business. The IAB reports that dynamically inserted ads account for more than 90 percent of podcast ad revenue, and publishers lean on mid-roll slots more than any other position, because an ad in the middle reaches people who have already committed to listening. Every marker is inventory, and inventory is something a sales team gets paid to sell.

### On video, the machine is watching

YouTube makes the logic easier to see. Since 2020, any monetized video longer than eight minutes can carry mid-roll ads, and YouTube turned them on by default for eligible videos. In 2025 it changed how it picks the spots, moving ads toward pauses and transitions and away from the middle of a sentence, and it told creators that channels using its automatic slots alongside their own earned more than 5 percent more ad revenue in testing. That's a real improvement for viewers, and it also means software is reading every long conversation to decide where the breaks go.

Then there's Peak Points. In May 2025, YouTube announced an ad format that uses Google's Gemini AI to find the most emotionally engaging moments in a video and place ads right after them, on the theory that people remember ads better when their attention is at its highest. So the suspicion that breaks are timed to emotional highs isn't paranoia. On at least one major platform it's a product with a name, pitched to advertisers as a feature.

One more piece of research is worth knowing. In a randomized experiment with about 35 million listeners on Pandora, the music-streaming service, over 21 months, economists found that people react mostly to the total number of ads per hour, that their long-run sensitivity was three times what a month-long test would have shown, and that heavier ad loads pushed more listeners to pay for ad-free subscriptions. Ads don't only fund the free tier. They can be the reason to buy your way out of it.

> Ads don't only fund the free tier. They can be the reason to buy your way out of it.

**In short**

- Most podcast ads are inserted at download, so the same episode can carry different ads for different people, and old episodes get refilled.
- YouTube's software picks the break points, and its Peak Points format is designed to place ads right after a video's most emotional moments.
- A long Pandora experiment found heavier ad loads push people toward paying for ad-free tiers.

## Chapter Four: Free, Brought to You By

*The hero story and the ad sales desk.*

A lot of the biggest independent shows tell a version of the same origin story. They aren't a network, they don't charge you, nobody tells them what to say, and they're proud of it. Some of that is true and genuinely valuable. What the story tends to leave out is who sells the ads, and how that seller gets paid.

Take the most popular podcast in America. In 2020, Spotify signed The Joe Rogan Experience to an exclusive deal reportedly worth more than $100 million. When the deal was renewed in early 2024, the show returned to other platforms and Spotify took on the job of selling its ads everywhere it appears, including on YouTube. The Wall Street Journal reported that the new deal could be worth up to $250 million, with a minimum guarantee up front and a share of ad sales on top. In 2026 the partnership was renewed again on similar terms, with Rogan and his small team keeping editorial control.

That arrangement can be completely fair and still change the incentives. Editorial control means nobody tells the host what to talk about. It doesn't necessarily mean the host decides how many minutes of each hour are for sale, and a revenue share means everyone involved earns more when there's more to sell. The show stays independent in what it says, while the way it's sold starts to look a lot like a network's.

### The price of free

Calling a show free is accurate about money and quiet about attention. You pay in minutes and in focus, and an advertiser pays the show for the chance to borrow both. The writer Cory Doctorow coined a blunt word, enshittification, for the pattern this can follow on platforms: first they're good to their users, then they squeeze those users to please business customers, and then they squeeze the business customers too. Podcasting isn't a single platform, but the first two steps will feel familiar to anyone who has watched a favorite show's breaks grow.

It's only fair to say the other side plainly. Most shows aren't rich, ads pay for editors, producers and studios, and a host who takes sponsors is offering a trade that listeners can accept or walk away from. The frustration isn't that shows make money, it's that the hero story and the ad cadence don't match, and a show can't present itself as the antidote to network television while running the same playbook.

### Who gets what from a free hour

- **The host.** Gets paid: Guarantees, revenue shares and sponsor fees Wants more of: A bigger audience, and enough ad slots to hit the numbers
- **The ad seller.** Gets paid: A cut of every impression sold Wants more of: More inventory, sharper targeting and higher prices
- **The platform.** Gets paid: Ad revenue, or a subscription to remove the ads Wants more of: Time spent, and a reason for you to upgrade
- **The advertiser.** Gets paid: Your attention, at a moment it can measure Wants more of: A slot right where you're most hooked
- **The listener.** Gets paid: Nothing, in money Wants more of: Fewer, clearer and more honest breaks

**In short**

- Independent editorial control is real, but it's a different thing from controlling how much of the hour gets sold.
- Revenue-share deals reward everyone in the chain for more ads and better-placed ones.
- Free means you pay in attention, and the hero story only holds if the ad load matches it.

## Chapter Five: The Sniff Test

*When a mention is really a deal.*

Not every ad sounds like an ad. The kind that tends to work best is the host read, where the person you already trust tells you about a product in their own voice, often with a personal story attached. Host reads made up about 46 percent of US podcast ad spending in the third quarter of 2025 by the count of Magellan AI, a firm that tracks podcast ads. In the first quarter of 2026, 2.45 percent of listeners who heard a host read went on to visit the brand's website, against 1.51 percent for produced spots, the pre-recorded ads that sound like radio commercials. The two shares overlap with the dynamic-insertion figure, because most host reads are now recorded separately and inserted the same way. They work because they borrow trust that the show spent years building.

Listeners are wary of exactly that. In a YouGov survey of British podcast listeners, only 13 percent said they trust podcast advertising. Asked which format they trusted most, 17 percent picked the host read, the top answer, and half picked none of them. People can tell when a friendly mention has a contract behind it, or at least they suspect it.

### What the law calls a material connection

US law has a name for the thing people are sniffing for. The FTC's Endorsement Guides, updated in 2023, require anyone endorsing something to disclose a material connection, meaning any relationship that could change how much you trust what they say. That includes payment, free products, affiliate commissions, equity, employment and family or business ties, and it applies no matter how small the perk is. On a podcast the disclosure is supposed to be spoken in the episode itself, not tucked away in the show notes.

Some shows skip most outside sponsors and promote their own products, events and services instead, which is more honest in one obvious way, because everyone knows the host owns the thing being pitched. It can still get cheesy, and it can still blur. When a guest is also a partner, an investor or a sponsor of the host's own conference, a conversation that sounds organic may carry a relationship the audience never hears about.

A case involving the company behind the PBD Podcast shows why the test reaches beyond the show itself. Patrick Bet-David's media company, Valuetainment, also runs a yearly business conference called the Vault and a consulting arm, and in early 2025 he told Mediaite he had paused sponsorships a year earlier at a cost of about $6 million, and would only take sponsors whose products he actually uses. That August, Goliath Ventures announced itself as title sponsor of the 2025 Vault, in what its own press release called a partnership with Valuetainment and Bet-David Consulting. The two don't necessarily conflict, since his comment was about sponsors on his shows and this was a conference sponsorship arranged through his companies.

According to federal prosecutors, Goliath was run as a Ponzi scheme from 2023 to 2026, and the government says it has identified at least $400 million paid in by investors; the SEC's civil complaint alleges at least $425 million raised from more than 1,300 investors. Court documents describe luxury events and charitable sponsorships among the tools used to make the firm look legitimate to investors. Its founder, Christopher Delgado, pleaded guilty in June 2026 to conspiracy to commit wire fraud, wire fraud and money laundering, and admitted investor losses of at least $250 million. His sentencing, first set for October 8, 2026, is now scheduled for January 28, 2027, according to the US Attorney's Office. Company records obtained by WFTV list a $1 million payment from Goliath to Bet-David Consulting, recorded as a donation. According to court filings, in April 2026 the Goliath bankruptcy estate, which operates under court supervision, served Bet-David with a subpoena, a legal demand for his records on the relationship.

A subpoena is a request for records rather than an accusation, and nothing reported so far shows that he knew the money came from a fraud. Coffeezilla, who questioned him about it publicly, has said there's no evidence of that. The lesson is about the audience, who saw a sponsor's name on a trusted stage and had no way to see what sat behind it. It's also a reminder that a show can step back from ad reads and still carry sponsorship money somewhere else in the business, so a mostly in-house model isn't automatically a clean one.

> A warm mention is only as honest as what sits behind it.

### The sniff test, in seven questions

Run these whenever a mention sounds a little too warm. Tap a box to tick it off; your ticks stay on this device.

**Was it disclosed out loud?** When a connection exists, a spoken "this is a sponsor" or "I'm an investor" is required. A clear disclosure is a good sign.

**Does the host or guest own a piece of it?** Equity and partnerships count as material connections, the same as cash.

**Is there a code or a link?** Discount codes and affiliate links usually mean someone is paid for every sale.

**Has it come up in several episodes lately?** A run of warm mentions in the same month is often a campaign.

**Is the guest a sponsor somewhere else?** Check the host's events, conferences and other shows.

**Would the host ever criticize it?** Praise that never comes with a downside is a pitch.

**Is the money claim too good?** Guaranteed returns, steady monthly income and can't-lose language deserve the most suspicion of all.

**In short**

- Host reads are now the biggest podcast ad format and, by most measures, the best performing, because they borrow the host's trust.
- The FTC requires spoken disclosure of any material connection, including equity, perks and business ties.
- Promoting your own products is more honest than outside sponsors, but Goliath's announced sponsorship of the Vault conference shows that even a named sponsor can hide what's behind it.

## Chapter Six: Fighting Back

*Skip, block, pay, and what happens when you do.*

Listeners already push back in small ways every day. YouGov's 2026 US report found that about half of podcast listeners tune out or skip ads, even though podcast ads still rank as the least annoying ad format in its surveys. Here are the main tools, roughly from the least effort to the most.

- **Skip by hand.** Most podcast apps let you set the skip-forward button to 30 or 60 seconds, which clears most breaks in a tap or two.
- **Let the crowd skip for you.** SponsorBlock is a free, open-source browser extension where viewers mark the sponsor segments in YouTube videos so everyone else's player jumps past them. Its public counter shows millions of marked segments and thousands of years of combined time saved, and a sister project does the same for Spotify podcasts in the browser.
- **Clean the feed.** Services such as ZeroAds, and self-hosted tools such as Podly, download episodes, find the ads with transcription and AI, cut them out and hand you a private ad-free feed for your usual app. They cost money or setup time, and they work on open podcast feeds rather than inside closed apps like Spotify.
- **Pay for the quiet.** YouTube Premium removes YouTube's own ads, and together with YouTube Music it passed 125 million subscribers, including trials, in 2025. Since January 2025, Spotify Premium subscribers can watch video podcasts from shows in its Partner Program, the scheme that pays creators for video, without the ads that get inserted automatically. Neither one removes the sponsor reads a host records into the episode.
- **Pay the show directly.** Memberships, premium feeds and listener support fund the people you actually listen to. Dan Carlin built Hardcore History mostly without ads by keeping new episodes free and selling the back catalog, and he has said plainly that he doesn't enjoy being a pitchman.
- **Vote with your time.** The Pandora music experiment suggests audiences respond to ad load over months rather than days, so quietly listening less to the worst offenders is a signal that does register.

### What happens when you do

Every one of those tools pushes on the business, and the business pushes back. In 2023 YouTube started blocking playback for people using ad blockers unless they allowed ads or paid for Premium, and since then users with blockers have reported slowed videos and broken skip buttons, while Google maintains that blocking ads breaks its terms of service. The fight over who gets to skip is an arms race, and the platforms own the pipes.

The deeper effect is easy to miss. When listeners skip and block the ads that are easy to spot, money moves toward the ones that are hard to spot: host reads baked into the conversation, product placement, integrations and casual mentions that never get called ads at all. A tool can cut out a labeled break, but it can't reliably cut out a sincere-sounding recommendation, because it doesn't sound like an ad. Blocking on its own can push the whole medium toward the kind of selling that's hardest to see, which is exactly the kind the sniff test is for.

> Block the obvious ads, and the money moves to the ones you can't hear.

That's why the most effective pushback mixes all of it. Skip what's clearly labeled, pay directly for the shows you'd miss, reward the ones that keep their breaks short and honest, and hold everyone to clear disclosure, so the ads that remain are at least the kind you can see coming.

**In short**

- Listeners can skip by hand, use crowdsourced skippers, clean their feeds, buy ad-free tiers or pay shows directly.
- Platforms fight back, and blocking only the obvious ads pushes money toward hidden ones.
- The strongest response combines skipping, direct support and a demand for disclosure.

## Chapter Seven: A Market for Quiet

*What a show that respects your time would look like.*

The demand is real enough that the biggest companies are already competing for it. Spotify's pitch for video podcasts is built on giving paying subscribers an uninterrupted experience, aimed squarely at YouTube. YouTube's own paid tiers posted their largest quarterly gain in non-trial subscribers ever in early 2026. And the same researchers who found that fewer than one in ten listeners would accept more ads are now pointing podcasters toward memberships, paid tiers and their most loyal superfans as the next source of revenue.

The market clearly exists, so the harder question is what an honest, ad-light show would actually look like, because a show with no ads at all is rare, and most of the good options are really about fewer, clearer and better-placed ones.

- **Listener-funded first.** Memberships, premium feeds or a pay-what-you-like model carry the show, and the free version stays free with only a short, honest ask.
- **One break, up front.** If there's an outside sponsor, it's read once at the top and never cut into the middle of a story, so the conversation is never the thing being interrupted.
- **Publish the ad load.** A show that states its ad minutes per hour, the way food carries a nutrition label, makes its promise checkable and makes heavier shows explain themselves.
- **Own products, plainly labeled.** Promoting your own book, event or service is fair, as long as it's announced as yours and kept out of the conversation itself.
- **Every connection disclosed.** The hosts' and guests' stakes, investments and sponsorships get said out loud, every time, not only when someone asks.
- **No break at the peak.** If breaks have to exist, they go at natural endings chosen by people who care about the show, not by software hunting for the moment you're most hooked.

### Looking ten years out

The pressure is only going to grow. Software that can find the most emotional moment in a video, insert a different ad for every listener and score how well a host read lands will keep getting cheaper, and voice technology is getting good enough that a sponsor read the host never actually recorded may not sound any different from one they did. In that world the scarce thing is trust, and the shows that keep it will be the ones that can show, plainly and repeatedly, when they're selling and when they're just talking.

That's also the real opening for anyone who wants to build something different. Competing on silence alone is hard, because someone still has to pay for it. Competing on trust, with a published ad load, honest disclosure and a direct relationship with listeners, is a position the big ad-supported shows can't easily copy without giving up revenue they've come to depend on.

**In short**

- The demand for uninterrupted listening is real, and Spotify, YouTube and superfans are all paying for it.
- An honest show doesn't need zero ads, just fewer of them, labeled, up front and fully disclosed, with a published ad load.
- As ad tech and voice tools improve, trust becomes the scarce thing, and that's the best opening for anyone building something different.

## Epilogue: After These Messages

*Attention is the thing being sold.*

The old TV line, we'll be right back, was always a small promise. The show would return, and in exchange you'd sit through a few minutes of someone else's message. Podcasts started out by making a better promise, which was that you wouldn't have to wait at all, and for a long time a lot of them kept it.

Most still keep it better than television ever did, and that's worth remembering before writing off the whole medium. But the biggest shows have drifted toward the cadence they once mocked, the software deciding where breaks land keeps getting smarter about your attention, and the warmest mentions are sometimes the ones with the most behind them. None of that needs a villain. It only needs a business where your attention is the product and nobody is keeping count on your behalf.

> Your attention is the product, so somebody has to keep count.

So keep count. Notice the minutes, notice where the breaks fall, ask what's behind the warm mentions, and spend your money and your time on the shows that treat both with respect. The best conversations are still out there, and plenty of hosts would gladly let you listen without interruption if enough listeners made it worth their while.

### Words worth knowing

*The terms this book leans on, in plain English.*

- **Download and reach.** A download is one copy of an episode sent to a device, whether or not anyone plays it. Reach is the share of people who say they listened or watched in a given week or month. The two often rank shows differently.
- **Ad load.** The share of a show's runtime spent on ads, usually given in minutes per hour or as a percentage.
- **Dynamic ad insertion.** Adding ads to an episode at the moment it's downloaded or streamed, so different listeners can get different ads.
- **Pre-roll, mid-roll and post-roll.** Ads before, during and after the show. Mid-rolls sell best because the listener has already committed.
- **Host read.** An ad read by the show's own host, often in their own words. It can be recorded into the episode, called baked in, or inserted dynamically.
- **Impression and CPM.** One delivery of an ad to one listener, and the price advertisers pay per thousand of them.
- **Programmatic.** Ads bought and placed automatically by software rather than sold show by show.
- **Ad pod.** Two or more ads played back to back in a single break.
- **Peak Points.** YouTube's AI ad format that places ads right after a video's most engaging moments.
- **Material connection.** The FTC's term for any relationship, like pay, perks, equity or business ties, that could change how you weigh an endorsement.
- **Affiliate code.** A discount code or link that pays the person sharing it for each sale.
- **Enshittification.** Cory Doctorow's word for platforms that get worse for their users as they squeeze more money out of them.
