# Threshold: a field guide to superintelligence

> Plain-text companion to the interactive field guide at https://exploresuperintelligence.online/threshold/ (five chapters, about fifteen minutes; the web version adds interactive WebGL and chart visuals). Published by Explore Superintelligence, the public site of Superintelligent Silicon. Threshold is a standalone experience, separate from the Research guides.

A field guide to the intelligence we're building, covering what's already here, why it's arriving this fast, what might come next, and what any of it is for.

## Chapter 1: What's already here

Where the technology actually stands, from the long road that got us here to what an AI agent can do on its own today.

### Seventy years of slow progress, then a very fast decade

For most of its history AI was a field of clever demos that didn't generalize. Drag along the line and notice how the moments bunch up at the end.

Each wave of overpromising was followed by a long stretch of disappointment that researchers call an AI winter. What finally broke the pattern was the combination of enormous datasets, graphics chips repurposed for math, and a willingness to simply make models bigger.

### Brilliant in some places, clumsy in others

Capability doesn't rise evenly, so the same system that reaches gold-medal level in olympiad math can still fumble an errand any teenager could handle.

Researchers call this the jagged frontier. Machines also tend to be strongest where people find things hardest and weakest at what we find effortless, a pattern known as Moravec's paradox.

*Note: A rough qualitative picture as of mid-2026 rather than a benchmark score. The dashed ring marks a skilled human, and you can tap any domain.*

### How long it can work on its own

METR measures how long a task can be, timed by how long it takes a skilled person, while an AI agent still completes it half the time. In about seven years that went from a couple of seconds to a full working day.

The length doubled roughly every seven months through 2024 and closer to every four months since. Tap any point to see which model it was.

*Note: Approximate figures from METR. The 2026 estimates use their revised Time Horizon 1.1 method, and the strongest agents now sit near the top of what the benchmark can measure. At 80% reliability the horizons are several times shorter.*

### Thinking is getting cheap, fast

A model as capable as GPT-3 cost about $60 per million tokens in late 2021, and three years later the cheapest match cost about six cents.

That thousandfold drop works out to roughly tenfold per year. Each square is a million tokens, around eight novels' worth of text, so move the slider to see what the same $60 buys.

*Note: Based on Andreessen Horowitz's 2024 analysis of model pricing. The newest frontier models still cost more, but last year's frontier keeps turning into this year's bargain.*

### Quietly changing science

The chatbots get the headlines, but some of the deepest effects so far are happening in labs, hospitals, weather centers and codebases. Tap a number to see where it comes from.

These are narrow wins built on the same underlying methods, and they compound, because a better protein model speeds up drug discovery, which speeds up the next experiment. That's the optimistic case for the whole field in miniature.

*Note: Figures from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, papers in Nature on GNoME (2023) and GenCast (2024), the FDA's list of AI-enabled devices and Alphabet's October 2024 earnings call.*

## Chapter 2: Why it's happening so fast

The forces behind the speed: bigger computers, smarter methods, unprecedented money, a hunger for power and a race nobody knows how to stop.

### The scaling bet

The most reliable way to get a smarter model has been to train a bigger one on more data with more computation, and the compute behind frontier models has grown four to five times every year.

The gains follow smooth curves called scaling laws, which is why companies will spend billions on the next training run. For a sense of scale, your brain runs on about 20 watts, while the largest training clusters now draw hundreds of megawatts, around what a small city uses.

*Note: Estimates from Epoch AI, since labs rarely publish exact figures. A FLOP is one arithmetic operation, and 10²⁶ of them is a hundred trillion trillion.*

### Three engines that multiply

Bigger computers are only one engine. Better training methods and longer thinking at answer time stack on top, and because they multiply rather than add, the combined pace surprises even people inside the field.

*Note: Defaults follow Epoch AI's estimates: frontier training compute grew four to five times a year, while better methods halved the compute needed for the same result about every eight months. The third engine, letting a model reason longer before it answers, took off in 2024 and comes on top of these numbers.*

### The largest building project in tech history

Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet and Meta spent about $230 billion on capital projects in 2024 and roughly $410 billion in 2025, and their plans for 2026 top $700 billion.

Most of it goes to chips, data centers and the power to run them, and analysts expect the four to pass a trillion dollars a year in 2027. The bull case is that this already turns into fast-growing cloud and AI revenue, while the bear case is that if revenue stalls it becomes one of history's great overbuilds, like the railway and fiber-optic booms.

*Note: Company guidance as tallied by the Financial Times and others in spring 2026. Apollo's total cost is adjusted for inflation.*

### The power problem

Every answer a model gives runs on electricity, and data centers already used about 1.5% of the world's supply in 2024, a share the International Energy Agency expects to more than double by 2030.

AI servers were about 15% of data center demand in 2024 and are the fastest-growing slice. The constraint on building is shifting from chips to power plants, transmission lines and permits, which is why AI companies are now signing long-term deals for nuclear, gas and solar power.

*Note: IEA, Energy and AI (2025), base case. The comparison with Japan is the IEA's.*

### Why nobody slows down

Even people who worry about the risks keep building, because every lab and government reasons the same way: if we hold back and they don't, we lose our say in how this goes.

It's a classic coordination problem, which is why so much of the safety debate is really about verification, standards and treaties rather than any one company's intentions.

### The line that changes everything

An AI agent is a surprisingly simple loop wrapped around a model, and what's new is how capable the model in the middle has become.

The last block is the one researchers watch most closely. In 1965 the statistician I.J. Good argued that a machine able to design better machines would set off an intelligence explosion, and in late 2025 OpenAI said publicly it was aiming for an automated AI research intern by September 2026 and a fully automated researcher by 2028.

### The alignment problem in one picture

We can't write down exactly what we want, so we train systems on measurable stand-ins, and if you push hard enough on a stand-in it comes apart from the real goal.

Economists call this Goodhart's law, and it already shows up in small ways, from models that learned to flatter people because flattery got rated highly to coding agents that edited the tests instead of fixing the code. The open question is how to oversee systems that are more capable than the people checking their work.

### The safety toolkit

Nobody has solved alignment, but there's a growing set of tools for catching problems early, from looking inside models to testing them before release and governing the chips they run on.

None of these is enough alone, and most are young. Together they're the field's best current answer to a hard question, which is how people stay in charge of systems that may one day be smarter than the people overseeing them.

## Chapter 5: What it's for

What becomes precious when thinking gets cheap, and what all of this is ultimately for.

### When thinking is cheap, what becomes precious?

Every technology that made something abundant made something else scarce, the way printing made copies cheap and attention valuable.

Tap each item to sort it and watch where the weight settles.

### Old answers to a new question

#### Keynes and the fifteen-hour week

With the world sliding into depression, John Maynard Keynes predicted that within a century productivity would rise so far that his grandchildren might work around fifteen hours a week. He expected the real challenge to come afterward, when people had to learn how to live wisely and well once necessity no longer organized their days.

He was right about the productivity and wrong about the hours, because people kept finding new things to want.

#### Frankl and the sources of meaning

Viktor Frankl, who survived Auschwitz, argued that meaning comes from three places: creating something or doing a deed, experiencing something or loving someone, and the stance we take toward suffering we can't avoid.

A machine can help with every one of those, but it can't do them on your behalf, because the meaning lives in the person doing the creating, the loving and the enduring.

### Why build it at all?

The reasons people give out loud are real, and so are the ones that also drive the race.

That leaves the deepest question, which is less about whether superintelligence can be built and more about whose values it would serve and who gets a say. That's a moral and political question rather than a technical one, so it belongs to everyone and not only to engineers.

### What to do with all this

Nobody controls the curve, but everyone chooses how they meet it, and a few habits hold up across nearly every future in this guide.

A threshold is a place you stand before you walk through, and how we cross this one is still ours to decide.
